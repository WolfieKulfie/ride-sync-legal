Why we built it

Everyone leaves together. That is usually where it ends.

A group ride is four or five different rides happening on the same road. One rider is there for the destination and wants to get there. One is there for the road and would happily take all day. Somebody has pulled over to film the corner everyone else just took. And somebody at the back is quietly riding at the edge of their comfort to keep up with people who are not thinking about them.

Everybody pretends this is fine. It mostly is, until it is not. The pack splits at a junction, phones go to voicemail, and half the group spends the afternoon guessing. The usual fix is to tell everyone to ride faster or slower than they want to, which is a strange thing to do to a day out.

We wanted a group ride that could hold together at four different speeds. Where you can stop and shoot for twenty minutes without becoming a problem, because everyone can see you stopped and can see you moving again. Nobody has to choose between their own ride and the group's.

Then there was a reason to hurry

On a ride we heard about, a woman came off her bike. The group kept going. One other rider stopped. Everyone else found out later, which means for a stretch of time that nobody has ever measured properly, she was on the side of a road with most of her group riding away from her, not out of malice, but because they simply did not know.

That is the part that is hard to get past. Not that anybody chose to leave her. That nobody had to choose, because nobody was told. A group of riders had become a line of people each assuming somebody further back had it covered.

An SOS nobody receives is the same as an SOS nobody sends.

So the SOS in Ride Sync is not a button that calls somebody. It is a button that makes it impossible for the rest of your group to not know. Every screen in the ride goes red. It reaches phones directly, even if the app was closed. If there is no signal where you are, it waits and goes the moment a bar comes back. And the rider on the ground can see who has said they are coming, which matters more than it sounds when you are sitting in gravel wondering if anyone noticed.

What it can and cannot do

It will not stop a crash. Nothing on a phone will. What it changes is the twenty minutes afterwards, which is the part that was broken.

It also changes something quieter. When everybody in the group gets the alert at the same time, riding on becomes a decision somebody makes rather than something that just happens. We think most people, given the information, turn around. The problem was never that riders are callous. It is that a group moving at speed is very good at not noticing.

Why group riding, and not another ride tracker

There are good apps for recording a ride and comparing it with your own last one. We did not think the world needed another. The gap we kept running into was the one nobody builds for: the ride itself, while it is happening, with other people in it.

Starting a riding group has never been easier, and a lot of people do it without much thought about what they have taken on. If you organise a ride, you have taken on some responsibility for the people on it, and for everyone else using the road that day. We would like the tooling to make that easier to live up to rather than easier to avoid.

Who is building this

Ride Sync is made by Rev Republic, a small studio in India. The first line of code was written on 3 August 2026. It reached the Play Store after roughly a hundred builds and no funding at all, which is why it does one set of things properly rather than forty things adequately.

It is free, it has no advertising in it, and there is nothing to subscribe to. If that changes we will say so here before it changes anywhere else. Built in India, for riders anywhere, because the problem is not regional.

The app has a button for telling us it is broken, and every message gets read by a person who can actually fix it. That is not a support policy so much as the size of the team.
